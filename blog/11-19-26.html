A Minimalist's Guide to Divorcing Your Phone

11/19/2026

Note: You can skip straight to the tips below, but the introduction will give you a much better understanding of their idealistic intent. Additionally, this is agnostic of any specific brand or make of mobile device, but I may make more frequent references to Apple devices because I use one.

Table of Contents

  1. Preamble and Philosophy
  2. Methodology
  3. Techniques
    • The Home Screen
    • Push Notifications
    • Social Media

Preamble and Philosophy

I, like many others I'm sure, have grown ever more disillusioned with smartphones, social media, and the increasing role always-online tech plays in our lives. I'll preface this with the fact that I am a programmer and life-long tech enthusiast, and like to think that I hold a more nuanced perspective than "phones are bad," or "new technology is ruining society." I have many thoughts about the effects of modern tech, but I'll keep this specific to phones and social media. This is of course all my opinion and I'm not a psychologist, nor have I read much research on the topic, although I'd like to believe formal studies would back up my anecdotes.

I believe our current relationship with smartphone technology to be fundamentally broken, primarily driven by the utterly predatory design of these devices. This is a problem that has taken years to propagate; a slow burn fueled by big tech and social media alike, namely Apple, TikTok, Meta, and X. I imagine where we're at today isn't the endgame of some mastermind plan by Steve Jobs or Mark Zuckerberg all those years ago, but rather a monumental pile of opportunistic choices in favor of the Attention Economy at the expense of the user. It is years worth of greed under the guise of progress toward some unknown ideal.

The topic of smartphone usage has been scrutinized for decades at this point, but it amazes me how little has come of that. The discussion never seems to make it past basic statistics of screen time and detrimental social effects. These are certainly the end result, but the causes are what matter.

So what is the current state of things? Let's take a step back for a moment and talk metaphysically (this will be important later for designing the way you want to use your smartphone from first principles).

What is a phone? Seriously, what purpose does it serve, both in your life and the life of others? Why do we even call it a "phone"? Obviously our original conception of the smartphone was born out of the plastic brick flip-phones we used to carry around everywhere, in turn evolving from pagers, and so forth, the point of this being that these were devices designed to contact or be contacted by anyone at any time (innovative of course but rather double-edged when you think about it).

Ignoring earlier prototypes, Blackberry established the basic form factor with minimal functionality before Apple enshrined the current model of mobile design language in 2007 with the first iPhone (The Model T of its kind, in a way. It's amazing that a user back then could pick up an iPhone 18 today and immediately be able to use it). This was an incredible piece of tech for its time; you can now call, text, take photos, listen to music, browse the internet, and play games all on one portable device, with virtually infinite extensibility via the App Store. Wow, it almost kind of sounds like... a computer?

Your "phone" is just a mobile computer, with the added bonus of having a cell antenna built into it (which is used for internet connectivity more than anything else). That's it. You should think of all your interactions with it through this lens.

Okay, it's a computer, so what? Consider the following. You carry with you one of the most powerful computing devices ever designed 24-7-365. It fits in your pocket, everyone owns one, and if you don't have one you can go get one for $50 at your local tech store. You can access any piece of information virtually anywhere in the world, have a real-time face-to-face conversation with someone on the opposite side of the globe, and broadcast a photo or video of anything to the entire world instantaneously. It is straight out of mid-century science fiction. Imagine the power that kind of technology would have afforded an empire, or even a single man, in antiquity? In every sense you would think it would be the makings of a utopia, a panacea to the world's problems, and yet it feels like we're worse off than before.

The often-cited example of giving a child a power tool comes to mind. You don't give a child carte blanche with a chainsaw because they lack the physical strength or presence of mind to use it properly. We as a collective are that child, but we never had that loving adult to withhold the things we were not ready for, or carefully teach us the proper way to use them and the responsibility one must bear with that.

What became of our blind wandering? We are hopelessly addicted to our dopamine machines. They have been designed to infiltrate our lives and occupy every spare shred of time and attention. What's the first thing you do when you wake up? You probably check your phone, look at your notifications, and maybe scroll social media. What do you do on your commute to work? You're probably using it to listen to music or a podcast, checking traffic, maybe sending a quick text while you're driving or scrolling Reels on the train. What do you do when you're at work? Probably checking your phone throughout the day out of boredom, maybe respond to someone's text or something they sent you. Went to the bathroom? More scrolling. Your lunch break? Probably scrolling and responding to things. The commute home is the same. You get home tired from a long day and what do you do? Probably sit on the couch and scroll. Maybe put on a movie but get bored of it and scroll anyway. The last thing you do before bed? More likely than not checking your phone.

This is not an exercise in shame, rather in awareness. We are all attached to these things as if they were our fifth appendage, which is insane because none of this existed twenty years ago. How much of our days and consequently our lives do we spend in a mindless trance consuming garbage? I'm not going to bother citing screen time statistics, but they are truly astounding.

How many times have you opened an app looking to do something specific, were immediately confronted by your Home feed, then realized you completely forgot what you were even trying to do half an hour later. This is bad design. The application is actively distracting you from using it the way you initially intended. It's predatory and greedy. Remember, your phone is a portable computer. Imagine, every time at work when you tried to accomplish something the program flooded your senses with short videos designed to interest you about a fascinating topic that ultimately teach you nothing. It's like trying to taking an exam in Times Square.

I've found the result of all this to be a less-human world. Our minds and bodies grow weaker, we grow farther apart from one another, and our physical world slowly decays around us. I think if you ask most people they'd probably say they'd rather use their phone less and do other things more, but it never happens. We go marching on in our collective fugue state.

Do we have a choice? Of course we do, which is hopefully why you're here.

Methodology

When deciding whether or not to cut things out of my life I often follow this basic principle: if you don't miss it, you didn't need it. It doesn't have to be an all-or-nothing affair. Try going without it for a week. If that feels good, try a month, and so forth, with the knowledge you can always return to the thing. The important part is that you give yourself the chance to fully separate from it. Only when you break the habitual cycle can you accurately determine if it added value to your life. The decision to return to it must come from the conscious mind, not simply a subconscious craving out of habit or addiction.

I don't want this to come off as one of those self-help resources you see on your YouTube or Reels feed, something to the tune of "RECLAIM YOUR ATTENTION TODAY!!" or "I quit social media for 30 days. Here's what happened..." (paradoxically those are thirsting for your attention equally so). You can love this advice or hate it, it doesn't really matter to me either way. I would implore you, though, if you do decide to try this, don't post about it, don't brag about it, don't go out of you way to tell anyone. To flaunt self-improvement is to miss the point, and to turn anti-tech into a trend on X or TikTok is completely antithetical to its purpose. Don't contribute to the global media frenzy. Simply enjoy your existence as a human in the world sitting right in front of you.

With these ideas in mind, here are the techniques I've found to be successful for rebuilding your relationship with your phone.

Techniques

The Home Screen

The Problem

Your home screen is the first thing you see when you open your phone, and immediately so much has gone wrong. Despite whatever aesthetic changes you've added over the years with new theming and customization features, your home screen probably resembles the one Steve Jobs unveiled nineteen years ago: a chaotic vomit of app icons. Choosing the app you intend to use is an exercise in muscle memory, a digital analogy to remembering where you left your jacket. In some ways liking this or not is a matter of preference, but I see it as an unnecessary holdover from an earlier time. Do you really want to emulate those who used to cover their PC desktop in shortcuts they haven't opened in a decade?

The issue here is more than aesthetics though. Firstly, forcing you to display every single program installed on your device is obviously restrictive and poor design. It creates an arbitrary and unnecessary trade-off between form and function. Secondly, the tactile nature of the touchscreen is a major component of the addictive feedback loop. Let's say you open your phone to check the weather app which you have situated on the front page. But of course you also have Instagram on the front page, as it's an app you use often. It's familiar logo and sleek colors entice your monkey brain as it remembers the dopamine hit it receives when you tap it... and now you find yourself scrolling again. Every usage of your device is a temptation into distraction. Luckily, mobile OS manufacturers have since added "app library" features allowing you to choose what apps to display.

The Solution

  1. Remove all apps from your home screen except those you use daily or would require quick access to in a given situation (e.g. bank or transportation apps). Consider every app you currently have and think, "Do I need this?" and "Do I use this?". Uninstall any apps you haven't used in a while; you can always reinstall them in a few seconds. Your home screen -- especially the front page -- should be only the most core, basic essentials. Don't feel the need to be attached to any of the pre-installed system apps either, if you use it, keep it. If you don't, ditch it.

  2. Now that your home screen is clean and organized, you may ask, "How am I going to access all those apps I just got rid of?" I'm glad you asked because you have access to one of your phone's most powerful features: the search bar. On modern iOS versions this can be conveniently accessed by swiping down while on the home screen, or with the dedicated button near the bottom of your screen. Need to use an app? Just search for it. No more muscle memory addiction from seeing flashy icons. No more opening apps out of boredom. Even better, the search bar is also available on the lock screen by swiping down, meaning you can bypass the entire first step of having to unlock your phone. The search functionality has gotten quite good over the years, allowing you to search apps, photos, messages, and the internet all from one place. So use it!

  3. This one is a little more on the extreme side, and thus more of an optional thing. Apple now has a standardized feature to alter app icon theming and colors (for dark mode etc.). Specifically, this allows you to make all icons either transparent and uncolored, or some range of monochromatic. This is a great way to further break the addictive formula and make your phone more boring (plus you can try to match it to your wallpaper).

The idea behind all of this is friction. Every user interface has some level of it, and mobile designers have tried to streamline away every ounce of it. By reintroducing a small amount of it -- for example, by having to search for Reddit instead of opening it instantaneously -- you might make it difficult enough to get to your compulsion that your brain gives up and decides it's not worth the effort.

Push Notifications

The Problem

Not only how you use your phone is being manipulated, but when. It sends you a little buzz at the whim of any app for any reason, "Hey! Pay attention to me!," which is annoying in and of itself, but of course careens into a chain reaction of distraction. Your phone vibrates, you read the notification that was almost certainly not important enough to warrant looking at it, then you're checking everything else you've missed in the past twenty minutes, and now you're reading lengthy group texts or scrolling again. Allowing ourselves to be interrupted for any reason and at any time is one of the worst things we've normalized in the 21st century.

The Solution

  1. Turn off notifications for the apps that you don't need or want. This can either be done in your phone's notifications settings (which can usually get pretty granular) or in the settings of the app itself, allowing you to customize what types of things it will notify you of. Trying to do this for every app you have is pretty daunting, so I'd suggest doing it on a case-by-case basis. Every time you get a notification you consider to be annoying, unimportant, or redundant, stop it from happening in the future. This might involve unsubscribing from all those useless newsletters in your email inbox or the channels on YouTube you never watch anymore.

  2. Use Do Not Disturb mode. I have this on 99% of the time, but might occasionally turn it off if I'm expecting an important message (and then immediately get annoyed by all the noise that's let through). There is nothing, barring an emergency, that requires your immediate attention pulled away from whatever you happen to be doing in a given moment. The notification tab should serve as a list of important things to check when you have the time, but never as a constant distraction throughout the day. Be notified of things on your own schedule. Train of thought is sacred, treat it as such.

  3. I typically keep my ringer off -- audio notifications are obviously disruptive to you and everyone around you -- but I could maybe see the argument that this forces you to keep your phone with you at all times to know if you're getting a call. The only time it would probably make sense to keep your ringer on is at home, provided all non-call notifications are silenced, allowing you to keep some distance from it. But then again most of the calls I get these days are spam...

Social Media

The Problem

This easily deserves its own essay but I'll keep it to a terse summary. There is so much that is awful about social media platforms: disinformation, AI garbage, political echo chambers, sophistry, pseudo-intellectualism, FOMO. Ultimately I believe social media, short-form content, and infinite-scroll feeds are the origin of so many of our modern social ills. It is easily the most addictive feature of your phone and probably has the biggest share of your screen time. How much of your day do you spend flooding yourself with overwhelming amounts of useless information and stimuli? Again, it's like spending your free time in Times Square. Even then I think hanging out in the actual physical Times Square would be more productive than its digital counterpart. These platforms are vast and unending; they quickly grow beyond our comprehension. The omnipresent black-box algorithm decides what you're feeling yesterday, today, and tomorrow.

The Solution

There's no one-size-fits-all solution here. I've almost entirely quit social media, I no longer post or check anything related to my personal life, and rarely check any of the big platforms (although I still frequent YouTube every day). There's no reason you have to do the same, but I'd certainly recommend quitting short-form content and limiting your other usage in general. When I do engage, I've found longer-form text-based forums to be the most harmless (i.e. Reddit. X/Bluesky can be okay if you follow the right people but are lethal in high doses).

To quit I found initial success through setting screen time limits (first an hour then 30 minutes), and eventually just deleting the apps entirely. There's no temptation to use it if you don't have them in the first place. A more in-depth guide into quitting would really be a deep dive into addiction itself. What's important is that you don't isolate yourself or drop communication with friends and family as a result. This is a chance to be more connected with people. Give someone a call. Plan to meet up in person. Stick to dedicated communities with a specific focus. Share content meaningfully and intentionally with others, have a discussion about it. Separate yourself from the mass psychosis.

No Phone in Bed

The Problem

Using your phone in bed is a bad habit for so many reasons. Blue light will keep you awake longer at night and scrolling in the morning delays the time before you start your day, plus the proximity increases the temptation to keep snoozing your alarm.

This is a hard and fast rule. Honor your bed as a sanctuary for sleep.

The Solution

  1. Don't use your phone in bed.

  2. Keep your phone on the opposite side of the room at night. This will force you to get up in the morning to turn your alarm off, or better yet just use an old-fashioned alarm clock.

Intentionality

The Problem

Much of what I've discussed so far can really be summed up as a problem of intentionality. You should use your phone -- and really, live your life in general -- with intentionality. This means doing the thing you intended to do without getting distracted. It means having a reason to use your phone instead of checking it at the slightest touch of boredom or an idle moment.

The Solution

  1. Be mindful. That's really all it takes. Recognize the urge to check your phone out of boredom. You don't always have to resist this urge per se, but recognize it and consider whether that's really what you should do, or if you should just appreciate the scene around you.

  2. If you opened your phone to do something specific, do that thing. Don't get distracted, and don't let it take more time than it needs to.

  3. Ignore non-relevant sources of information and stimulation.

  4. Find meaningful ways to fill the longer gaps in your day, whether it's reading or watching something truly entertaining or educational.

Conclusion

Congrats! If you've followed any of the steps above you likely gained back some of the time in your day -- at worse a few minutes and at best even a few hours. These voids must be filled with something or you'll quickly fall into old habits. Breaking a bad habit is only the first victory in a long war against your subconscious tendencies. This is now a chance to do that thing you always wanted to do. If you've quit scrolling after getting home from work, consider reading that book or starting that show you've always wanted to. If you've simply quit the small periods of boredom scrolling throughout your day, enjoy the short respites that life naturally offers you. Remember the point of all this is not to min-max your life. There is no rush, no urgency, only that which we fabricate in our minds. The goal is to live well and healthy, not for a day but for a lifetime.
